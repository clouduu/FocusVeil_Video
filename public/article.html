ABSTRACT

The Limits of Sustained Attention

R. Chen, M. Alvarez · Journal of Attention Science, 2024

Attention is a limited resource, allocated across the competing streams of information that arrive at any given moment. When the demands of a task exceed the capacity available to process them, performance does not degrade smoothly, but in a cascade of small failures. The eyes drift away from the line being read. Participants report losing track of the sentence already half-read. Regressions — backward saccades to passages with ambiguous grammar or intricate phrasing — do not signal failure alone, but reveal the ongoing calibration of effort. Both interest in the material and the cost of extracting meaning shape how working memory is distributed while reading. Comprehension is not simply a matter of decoding sequences, but of constructing a coherent model of the text as participants read.

Reading is not a single act but a chain of small, tightly timed decisions: where to look next, how long to hold, when to move on. Each decision costs a fraction of attention, and neither is unlimited. Under load the chain frays at its weakest link, and the reader is left reconstructing meaning from a sequence they no longer quite remember. Comprehension depends on the reader being able to hold the beginning of a sentence in mind while the end of it arrives, and on returning to the same place twice only when the text demands it. Both conditions fail quietly. Nothing announces the failure; the reader simply arrives at the bottom of the page with less than they started. Reading is not robust to distraction. A second study manipulated line length and column width: narrower measures reduced regression frequency by 16 percent without cost to reading speed, but only for participants reporting high trait inattention.
